The largest surface on screen is the one nothing draws.

Two lines of DayNight paint the sky: one flat colour, and the fog set to match it. No horizon, no sun, no moon, no stars, no cloud — and at the camera's play pitch, no sky on screen at all. What ships today

Captured from the running game at six hours with the camera tipped up (scripts/sky-evidence.mjs). The measured scene.background is printed on each frame. There is no second colour anywhere in any of them.

The whole implementation

DayNight.update() lerps between three colours on daylight and assigns the result twice:

scene.background = sky · fog.color.copy(sky)

That is it. The sky is a clear colour, which is the same thing as not drawing a sky.

Why every dusk is brown

daylight is one number that peaks at noon, so 05:30 and 19:30 are the same value and therefore the same colour. A morning that comes up blue-violet and an evening that goes down orange-rose cannot both be described by it, so neither is: they average into the tan you see at 07:00 and 18:00 above.

The ramp below is keyed on the clock instead, which is why it can tell them apart.

Where the sky actually is

The flat colour is only half the problem, and the smaller half. At the camera's play pitch there is no sky on screen at all — the band that reads as sky is fogged sea and island. It is indistinguishable today because the fog is set to the same colour as the background.

The arithmetic

The rig looks down at the player from pitch degrees above them; the camera's vertical field of view is 35°. So the top of the frame points pitch − 17.5 degrees below horizontal, and a flat sea's horizon sits at exactly 0°.

Proof, from the running game

Both frames are noon at pitch 20. The second one has the fog pushed out past the ocean plane and changes nothing else.

Everything that was "sky" turns back into island and sea. What is left of the real sky is the strip along the very top.

The full sky is drawn in all three. You cannot see it in the first two because it is not in the frame — and in the third it is a strip along the top. This is with the rework working.

Three consequences, and they set the whole plan.

1 · The fog colour is the sky for almost every frame the player sees. The ramp has to drive the fog, or the rework is invisible in play. That is Phase 1, it is cheap, and it changes every frame in the game.

2 · A moon, a cloud and a star can only appear above the horizon, and the play camera never points there. Without a camera change, recipes B and C are cutscene features.

3 · So the sky needs somewhere to be looked at. The cheapest is the camera the game already has: the bodies now ride low on purpose (36° and 26°), and a view tilted up ~25–35° frames them. Tie that to something the player already does — sitting on a bench, standing at the dock's end, or the idle drift going one step further — and the sky becomes a place rather than a backdrop. The vista frames below are that camera.

The model

Back to front. Everything except the stars is one dome, one shader, no textures — a cloud is a threshold on a noise field and the sun is an angular disc, so every edge lands on the same 480×360 grid as the rest of the frame and quantises with it.

One day, recipe C — at 12° pitch

Below the game's play pitch, deliberately: at 45° and 20° a strip of six frames would be six pictures of grass. What these show is the ramp — morning comes up cool and blue, evening goes down warm and red, and today's sky cannot tell those apart.

The vista — the rig's framing, tilted up

The gaze differs per frame because the bodies do: the sun peaks at 36° and the moon at 26°, and each is only near the middle of the frame at the hour it crosses the north. The sun sets in the west, off the side of a 45°-wide frame — which is its own argument for letting the player turn the camera as well as tilt it.

The three recipes, and today, at 18:36

A · Painted ramp

The dome and the dither, nothing else. Fixes the flatness and the morning/evening collapse; adds no objects to look at.

Take it if the jury wants the sky to stop being a bug without the sky becoming a feature.

B · Bodies and stars

A · plus a sun that is where the light comes from, a moon with a phase, and four constellations. This is the smallest version that gives the night a reason to be looked at.

Recommended landing — the first thing to wire into DayNight.

C · Cloud

B · plus the drifting band, tied to Weather's cover, wind and storm. The one layer with a real chance of reading as noise at this resolution, which is why it is last.

Target, gated on B landing well.

Stars worth naming

Rule 4 — generate for coverage, author for intent — bites hardest here: a random point field is what every prototype sky already has, and nobody has ever pointed at one. Four shapes a player can find twice, with names that belong to a ferry island rather than to Greek myth. The filler between them is seeded, so tonight's sky is tomorrow's sky.

What it unlocks later: a star chart is data, not decoration — CONSTELLATIONS is addressable, so "look at the Ferry tonight" is a quest step, a museum drawing is a collectible, and a shooting star is an event with an id. None of that is in scope here; the point is that this shape does not close the door on it.

The plan

Four phases. Each lands something playable, each has a gate that is not "it looks nicer".

P0

Decide the camera question first

Nothing above the horizon is visible at 45° or at 20°. Either the idle drift's angle goes to ~14° — a tenth of the frame becomes real sky the moment the player stands still — or the sky is accepted as a cutscene and horizon-band feature and B/C are scoped accordingly. One line either way; it changes what the rest of this plan is worth.

Gate: a human picks. The lab's pitch slider and its "sky above the horizon" readout are the tool for picking.

P1

Land the ramp — into the fog first, then the dome

DayNight takes its colours from skyAt(hour) instead of the three-stop lerp, and feeds the horizon colour to the fog. That alone changes every frame in the game, because the fog is what the player has been reading as sky. Then the dome goes in behind it, following the camera in GameLoop, and scene.background stops being assigned.

Gate: the sea still has no visible seam at the fog's far edge on every supported island; npm run test and the water gate stay green; the same six hours are back in the queue for comparison.

P2

Bodies agree with the light

DayNight's sun position and the dome's sun direction come from bodyDirections() — one source, so the shadow direction and the visible disc can never disagree. The moon's phase comes from the calendar's day.

Gate: a measured check that the shadow the ground receives points away from the disc in frame, at four hours, on two islands.

P3

Stars, and the night's reason to look up

Stars into the scene, opacity from daylight and Weather's cover. Shooting stars fire on the existing event bus so a later verb can listen. Chart lines stay off in play.

Gate: the four constellations are findable from a normal play camera at 23:00 without moving; the field is identical across two boots (seeded).

P4

Cloud, tied to weather

Cover and storm from Weather, drift from its wind vector, so an overcast sky and rain on the ground are the same weather. Interiors skip the dome entirely.

Gate: rain never falls from a clear sky and never fails to fall from a black one, checked at three intensities; no measurable frame-time change on the perf script.

Risks, and what the jury has to answer

Cloud noise reads as dirt

Value noise at 480×360 through a nearest upscale is the layer most likely to look like static rather than weather. It is Phase 4 and cuttable without touching the rest.

A gradient competes with the town

The current flat sky is inoffensive precisely because it is empty. A strong dusk ramp can pull the eye off the character; the pitch slider in the lab is there to check that at 45° as well as at 20°.

The horizon must stay the fog

If the dome's base and the fog colour ever drift apart, the ocean ends in a visible line. It is one uniform today and it must stay one number in one place.

Interiors and cutscenes

Interiors must skip the dome; scripted shots that swing the camera high will frame more sky than the game normally does, and the ramp was authored for the low band.

The stops are one person's eye

Eleven authored colours are a taste claim. They are cheap to change — one table — but they are the part most likely to be wrong, and no metric will say so.

Nothing here is approved

Per the jury rule this is built, framed and queued, not done. The runtime is unchanged until a human says the frames are right.

Four questions, the first one blocking: (1) Does the idle camera drop to ~14° so there is real sky on screen when the player stops — or does the sky stay a horizon-band and cutscene feature? (2) Recipe A, B or C as the landing target? (3) Is the dusk ramp too saturated at 20:12 — the frame where the proposal is furthest from what ships? (4) Should the moon have a phase at all, given the calendar's months are not synodic and it will not match the real sky outside the window?
