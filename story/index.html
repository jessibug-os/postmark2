Ferry to St. Mystere.

An ordinary first assignment becomes a crossing into an island that was decommissioned years ago—and a post office that is still waiting for someone to come back.

The opening promise

The player should begin in a recognizable world of jobs, ferries and assigned offices. The uncanny arrives gradually, and lands on a mourner. The funny human connection is Albert, and he is the first thing the player finds with her own two feet once the opening lets go of her — not the last thing a cutscene hands her.

What the player understands

  • They have accepted a first postmaster assignment.
  • They are traveling to a remote island called St. Mystere.
  • The office was closed years ago and nobody expected a new recruit.
  • The ferry route is ordinary until the island disappears into fog.

What the player feels

  • Curiosity during the recruit conversation.
  • Isolation as passengers leave one by one.
  • Dread when the ferry loses its normal world.
  • Grief, not relief, when the Keeper wakes: the light she carried inland turns out to be his, and he mistakes her for someone who never came back.

Beat sheet: ferry to fog

The sequence should feel authored and cinematic while keeping the player’s first input meaningful: name, assignment and the decision to follow the voice.

Boarding

The player boards a regional ferry carrying luggage, an assignment satchel and an acceptance letter. The camera gives a readable glimpse of the ordinary mainland harbor before departure.

The other recruit

A fellow recruit sits nearby. He is friendly, slightly overconfident and heading to his first posting. He asks the player’s name, then asks what island they were assigned to.

Player input

The name field and island field are framed as ordinary onboarding questions. The player types their name and enters St. Mystere as the assignment.

The warning

The recruit’s expression changes: “Whoa. I didn’t know they opened that office back up. It was decommissioned years ago.” He tries to laugh it off, then checks the route card twice.

His stop

The recruit reaches his island. He gathers his case, promises to write, and leaves the player alone with the other passengers. This is the last ordinary goodbye.

Time passing

Passengers get off at their stops in a gentle time-lapse. The ferry becomes emptier, quieter and less convincingly maintained. Small environmental details repeat incorrectly. The light goes with them: each stop runs the world clock forward and is cut tighter than the last, so the boat empties into a late afternoon rather than under a fixed sun. Nobody should notice it happening; they should notice that the boat got colder.

The mist

Outside the windows, the horizon vanishes. The engine sound stretches. The player hears a voice calling from somewhere ahead, loses consciousness and falls out of the ordinary timeline.

The dock

The player wakes alone on St. Mystere’s dock at dusk — the crossing has taken the afternoon with it. No ferry. No crew. No other passengers. A small pale light is burning at the end of the pier. It drifts a little way inland, and waits for her.

The walk inland

The player follows the light through an empty island — ninety metres of real input, the longest unbroken stretch of the opening. The wisp holds station ahead of her and waits when she falls behind, so the pace is hers. The path should be navigable but unnerving: abandoned signs, overgrown plots, a bell that rings without wind and no visible residents.

The spring

The light leads to the source of the river. Something is standing in the water and has been standing there a long time — a tall hooded shape hung with undelivered letters, with no light in it at all.

The Keeper wakes

The wisp goes into him and becomes the ember in his hood. He does not wake frightening; he wakes wrong about who she is — “you came back, I knew you would come back” — and then takes it back. The correction is the saddest moment in the sequence, because it tells the player there was someone before her who did not return.

The backlog

The Keeper of Undelivered Things explains what he is: when the office shut, everything undelivered came to him and he has held it since, because he did not know how to put any of it down. The people the letters were written for are still on the island, still waiting to be told they may go. He is only the place it stops. He asks her to carry it kindly forward, and points her down the hill to a door that has been shut a long time.

Dialogue targets

These are intent beats, not final script. The recruit and the Keeper are the two voices here, and they are not two kinds of warmth — they are warmth and its absence. Albert’s comedy has been moved out of the opening entirely: a joke three minutes before a mourner does not set him up, it inoculates the player against him.

Recruit · ordinary

“First assignment? You have the look. Don’t let the quiet get to you — every office is loud once it’s yours.”

Recruit · island question

“Which island did they send you to?”

Recruit · recognition

“St. Mystere? Whoa. I didn’t know they opened that office back up. It was decommissioned years ago.”

Recruit · departure

“This is me. Write when you get settled. People mean to. Then it gets hard to start, and then it gets too late.”

The Keeper · what he is

“I am the Keeper of Undelivered Things. When the office shut, everything that had not been delivered came to me. I have held it since. I did not know how to put any of it down.”

The Keeper · the backlog

“There are letters here older than the trees above us. The ones they were written for are still on this island, still waiting to be told they may go. And I am only the place it stops.”

Liliane and Vittorio

The cast no longer has two musicians. Liliane begins as an heiress whose musical training was another form of obedience; Vittorio is the musician who helps her discover a voice that belongs to her.

Her unfinished business

Liliane cannot accept that death may have ended the only life she was permitted to live. Her father chose her education, public image, marriage prospects and charitable work. She arrives with perfect manners and no practiced answer to “what do you want?”

Her letter is a final instruction from him, still demanding one more dutiful decision.

The shared arc

Vittorio does not rescue or cure her. He gives her a medium without an approved result: improvisation, bad notes, private rhythm and eventually a song they make together.

She moves from controlled performance to chosen expression. He moves from performing other people’s words to saying one honest thing of his own.

Tone control

The intro should not be uniformly spooky. Its shape is ordinary → wrong → eerie → grieving, and the absurdly human arrives after it, in Albert, once the player is playing.

The first game loop

The Keeper’s asking turns into a practical objective the moment the player is playing, and Albert — behind the door at the bottom of the hill — is the one who states it. The player does not meet a full village; they build the conditions for one.

  1. Restore the post office. Clear debris, patch the counter and light the sorting room.
  2. Gather island resources. Timber, stone, salvage, plants and tide-washed materials are useful before they are collectible.
  3. Prepare a home plot. The island cannot stabilize a summoned soul without a place prepared for them.
  4. Repair the summons ledger. Albert identifies one soul in the backlog whose business can begin on St. Mystere.
  5. Invite or summon that soul. The arrival is a small ceremony, not a menu confirmation.
  6. Welcome a villager. Their home, schedule, service, relationships and unfinished business become part of ordinary island life.
  7. Repeat with depth. Every new house makes the island less empty and the backlog more emotionally specific.

The mysteries we plant

The opening should answer enough to create trust while leaving three strong reasons to continue.

Immediate mystery

  • Why did St. Mystere’s post office reopen now?
  • Why was the Postmaster assigned to an office that was decommissioned?
  • Who was the Postmaster the Keeper mistook her for, and why did she not come back?
  • Why is Albert still behind the counter when everyone else is waiting?

Long mystery

  • What caused the island’s decay and the ferry backlog?
  • Where did the previous Postmaster and ferrymen go?
  • Did somebody stop the ferries deliberately?
  • Is the Postmaster alive, dead, or in transit?
Reveal discipline: do not make “the player was dead all along” the entire answer. The player’s metaphysical status should complicate her relationship with St. Mystere, not erase the residents’ stories or turn the opening into a twist puzzle.

First-slice acceptance test

Before a full narrative rewrite, the opening should prove these things in one playable hour.

  • The player names themselves and enters St. Mystere as their assignment.
  • The recruit’s decommissioned-office warning lands naturally.
  • The boat visibly empties and the fog transition feels earned.
  • The dock and walk to the office feel eerie without combat or a jumpscare.
  • The Keeper is immediately sorrowful rather than threatening, and his mistake about who she is lands.
  • The light she followed inland pays off as the thing that wakes him, rather than as a waypoint that switches off.
  • The backlog and ferry system are understandable without a lecture.
  • The first resource gathering and home plot make narrative sense.
  • The first summoned soul feels like a person arriving, not a content unlock.
  • The player understands why the island is empty and wants to make it inhabited.
