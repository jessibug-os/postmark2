Gameplay design · proposed authority

What the Postmaster does.

Kindly Forward is a cozy life-sim about walking somewhere with a purpose, noticing something on the way, and returning to people who remember that you went.

Preproduction, grounded in the current prototype. This document audits the existing Animal Crossing-adjacent systems and gives them a St. Mystere purpose. It does not add new item counts or replace the runtime robot cast yet.

The playable promise

The player is not selecting dialogue from a mail menu. They are a postmaster walking a physical island, carrying a fragile obligation through weather, water, schedules and ordinary distractions.

Minute-to-minute question

“What can I do with my hands, and who will notice that I did it?”

Every outing should combine one practical action, one observation and one social return: fish beside the river, notice a bridge candidate, bring the catch to a resident, then decide whether to sell it, donate it or give it away.

What makes it a game

  • Position, timing and attention matter during gathering.
  • Inventory space creates choices without punishing experimentation.
  • Letters require route planning and a physical handoff.
  • Residents respond to repeated behavior, not only menu selections.
  • Island restoration changes where the player can go and what a day can contain.

The minute-to-minute loop

A good ten-minute session has a small arc and a visible return. The player can stop after one catch, one delivery or one conversation and still feel they completed something.

Read the morning board.
Check the post office ledger, mailbox, weather, resident requests and today’s available letter routes. Choose one intention, not a mandatory checklist.
Pack the satchel.
Carry a tool, a few empty pockets and—if desired—a letter. Inventory pressure makes “take this now or come back later” a meaningful choice.
Walk the route.
Move through the island rather than teleporting between menus. The route exposes fish shadows, fleeing bugs, dig spots, drift mail, bridge candidates and residents in different places.
Make a physical attempt.
Cast and strike, swing before a bug flees, dig the marked ground, gather a flower, place furniture, survey a home plot or hand a letter to its addressee.
Choose the meaning.
Sell the catch for gears, donate it to Maeve’s archive, use it as a gift, save it for a recipe or keep it as a memory object. The same item supports different relationships.
Return changed.
A resident reacts, a letter advances, a building becomes possible, a collection gains a label or the player’s route becomes easier next time. Save at the post office or end the day at home.

Letters are a mechanic

Letters are the game’s signature verb because they turn walking, attention and social timing into one physical task. They are not a confirmation dialog with lore attached.

Three-stage delivery

  1. Claim: open the dead-letter drawer at the post office, inspect the address, sender clue, destination landmark and any delivery condition. The letter occupies a satchel slot.
  2. Route: carry it across the island. The player can gather and talk while carrying it, but cannot sell, discard or open it early. The address gives a reason to learn the map.
  3. Handoff: stand near the named resident or landmark and press the interaction key. The recipient reads it in a short scene, then the player receives a reaction, truth fragment or new choice.

Failure without cruelty

  • Wrong place: the game refuses the handoff and repeats the address clue; no item is lost.
  • Recipient unavailable: the resident leaves a note or the letter remains carried until their schedule returns.
  • Satchel full: the drawer cannot release another letter; the player must make room.
  • Player abandons route: returning the letter to the office is allowed, but the delivery does not advance and the day’s opportunity is spent.
  • Missed timing: weather or a festival can change the route, never permanently invalidate it.
Design rule: the player should be able to describe where they walked, what interrupted them, and why the recipient’s response mattered. If the delivery can be completed from a ledger screen, it is not the finished mechanic.

Core verbs, retained and re-authored

These are the current prototype’s Animal Crossing-adjacent activities, kept because they create useful reasons to walk—not because the robot game already had them.

Walk and notice

Current: WASD movement, camera exploration and proximity interactions.

St. Mystere: the primary verb. Routes reveal fog, tide, resident schedules, bridge candidates and changes in the island.

Fish

Current: cast near river, pond or ocean shadow; wait; strike during the bite window; fish vary by water, hour and season.

St. Mystere: a patient activity for Rowan and Vittorio, a source of gifts and archive specimens, and a way to read what the river is carrying.

Catch bugs

Current: swing the net; running close makes bugs flee; habitats and seasons gate species.

St. Mystere: quiet observation. Bugs should reward slowing down and give Mira, Niko and Maeve reasons to be outside at different hours.

Dive

Current: enter the ocean, chase moving shadows, press dive near a target, return to shore.

St. Mystere: a coastline-only verb for tidepool salvage and rare sea specimens. It never bypasses the river’s bridge gates.

Gather and dig

Current: shake fruit trees, pick flowers, dig marked spots, chop dead trees and collect timber, stone, scrap and relics.

St. Mystere: the material basis of homes, bridge surveys, post-office repairs and resident-specific spaces. Dig spots can also surface delayed mail.

Craft and upgrade

Current: recipes turn materials and gears into furniture, town pieces and better rod, net, shovel and axe.

St. Mystere: the player makes tools and places that express a resident’s new life—not an endless recipe treadmill. Upgrades widen routes or reduce friction.

Place and decorate

Current: carry furniture, rotate a ghost, place outdoors or in interiors, then pick it up again.

St. Mystere: player-authored homes and civic spaces. Placement affects comfort, resident routines and whether a soul can stabilize after summoning.

Archive and donate

Current: donate fish, bugs, sea life, fossils, art, forage, materials, relics, clothing and furniture to the Archive ledger.

St. Mystere: Maeve’s living archive records what the island was able to notice. Donation is not a sale with a prettier UI; it permanently changes the museum’s story and resident dialogue.

Shop and trade

Current: Reggie stocks rotating furniture, clothing and tools; the player sells found items for gears.

St. Mystere: the exchange that turns surplus into homes, letters, bridge materials and comfort. Stock should feel like requests from the village, not a random catalog.

Progression without a checklist treadmill

Progression should widen the player’s verbs and deepen their consequences. The player is never asked to complete every collection to be a good postmaster.

Early island

  • Repair the post office enough to claim and return letters.
  • Gather starter timber, stone, plants and tide salvage.
  • Choose the first home plot and summon one resident.
  • Learn fishing, netting, digging and furniture placement through necessity.

Growing island

  • Choose bridge sites from valid candidates and open new resource routes.
  • Build resident homes, clinic, archive, music space and shared amenities.
  • Unlock tool upgrades that make existing activities more expressive, not obsolete.
  • Use deliveries, gifts and archive donations to reveal resident choices.

Established island

  • Seasonal festivals remix familiar verbs: blossom gathering, regatta fishing, lantern routes and a winter performance.
  • Residency and friendship hearts unlock new letters, scenes and environmental changes.
  • Residents may cross, stay, postpone or return; none are removed from the player’s story for failing a hidden score.

Long tail

  • Complete collections for curiosity and Maeve’s archive, not mandatory progression.
  • Refine homes, bridges, gardens and civic spaces.
  • Replay days by changing routes, gifts, residents visited and which letter to carry.
  • Continue the village after the central mystery resolves.
What gates a letter (settled 2026-07-28): these four phases replace the runtime’s act counter. The prototype gated dead letters behind acts I–III plus a town-hearts threshold; Kindly Forward gates them on residency and build state instead. A resident’s letter becomes claimable when that resident lives on the island and the spaces their arc needs exist — you cannot deliver Mira’s letter before Mira is here. The gate is intrinsic to the fiction rather than a counter beside it, and it cannot deadlock: nothing is withheld pending a number the player might never reach. The Act I/II/III story spine in /island and /characters is narrative structure and is unaffected — what retires is the mechanical gate, not the three-act shape.

Verb-doc acceptance tests

Before this becomes implementation authority, a human should be able to play a short slice and answer these questions without explanation.

  1. Can the player leave the post office with a clear, optional intention?
  2. Can they perform at least three activities without opening a menu?
  3. Does a letter require carrying, routing and a physical handoff?
  4. Can a wrong address or unavailable resident create a soft failure without losing progress?
  5. Does the same fish, bug, flower or letter support at least two meaningful choices?
  6. Does one resident react differently because of what the player did, not only because a counter increased?
  7. Does building or bridge work change where the next day can go?
  8. Can the player stop after ten minutes with a completed small arc?
Success condition: Kindly Forward feels like a cozy life-sim with a postal vocation—walking, collecting, decorating, collecting stories and maintaining relationships—not a visual novel that happens to contain a fishing button.
