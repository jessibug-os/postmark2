A NEW START · THREE WORLD PREMISES

A life worth
moving into.

The player should want to come back even on a day when nothing important happens.

The earlier proposals began with an unusual action. This one begins with leaving home, finding your place among people who already know each other, and slowly making a life you chose.

My recommendation is Little Harbor: your first year living on your own in a small, busy ferry town.

WHAT MAKES THIS A WHOLE GAME

A home. A place in the town.
Something to look forward to.

A reason to be here

The player has made a recognizable life decision. Their home, money, relationships, and activities all follow from it. We should be able to explain the game without listing features.

People with their own lives

The town has friendships, work, arguments, traditions, and plans before the player arrives. Being welcomed into that world is part of the pleasure.

Progress you can live inside

A room becomes a home. An acquaintance saves you a seat. A job becomes something you are good at. There are reasons to return besides clearing another objective.

01 / MY RECOMMENDATION

Little Harbor

Your first address.
Your first job.
Your own way of living.

Who you are

You have just moved away from home. You picked Little Harbor because a room was available and somebody said there was work. You arrive with a suitcase, a modest amount of savings, and no particular idea of who you want to become.

You are not the mayor. You do not inherit the town. Nobody has been waiting for you to put their life back together.

Where you have come

A small ferry port on a wooded island, with a bakery, repair yard, café, boarding house, public bath, and a steep street leading up to the old observatory. The ferry brings commuters, relatives, visitors, and things the island cannot make itself.

The town is doing all right. Some shops are shabby. Some people want change. Others liked things better before the new timetable. There is work to do, but this is a functioning place you get to join.

Who lives here

A community of small, expressive, storybook people: sailors, shopkeepers, schoolchildren, retirees, and people who never quite settled on a profession. Build on the handmade, toy-like proportions already in the repo. They are living people with families and ordinary concerns; their bodies are not a metaphysical mystery.

There are long-established households, newcomers like you, and people who split their lives between the island and the mainland. You can be close to someone without becoming the center of their world.

What the game is about

Making an independent life, and deciding what belongs in it. Earn enough for the things you care about. Learn your way around. Make friends. Change your room. Discover a favorite place to sit. Eventually realize that someone else now considers you one of the locals.

THE FIRST EVENING

The town does not pause for your arrival.

You step off the ferry as a dockworker finishes her shift. She points you toward the bakery without putting down her crate. The baker shows you upstairs, gives you the spare key, and returns to an argument with his sister about who ordered twelve bags of rye flour.

Unpack three things in your room. Choose where your bed goes. Walk downstairs to buy something for dinner. The café is busy; someone asks whether the empty chair at your table is taken.

Before bed, put your name beside the doorbell. Tomorrow there are three places you could ask about work. Tonight, it is enough to have arrived.

THE EVERYDAY STRUCTURE

  1. Make a little moneyTake a short shift or help with a practical job.
  2. Spend the afternoon your wayMeet someone, explore, make something, or go home.
  3. Make your life more yoursChange your room, learn a skill, establish a routine.
  4. See what tomorrow bringsA visitor, a club meeting, a season, an invitation.

Work gives you a way in

Start with three modest sources of work: a café shift, neighborhood deliveries, and helping at the repair yard. Each introduces a different part of town. The café teaches names and preferences. Deliveries teach streets and households. The yard leads to making and repairing useful things.

Keep these jobs short and forgiving. The important choice is how you want to spend your day and whom you will see. No sprawling career trees and no requirement to master three unrelated minigames. You can leave a job without losing access to its people.

Your home makes earning worthwhile

Begin in one upstairs room. Furniture has a purpose: a table for inviting someone over, a shelf for your finds, a workbench for a hobby. You can change colors, arrange things, and put objects where they look right.

Later, choose a larger apartment or a small house. Neither needs to replace the room you love. Different homes offer different lives: a yard, a harbor view, a neighbor next door. Avoid a single biggest house that makes every other choice obsolete.

Leisure makes this a life

Go beachcombing, fish off the breakwater, tend a few pots, visit the bathhouse, or join the observatory’s evening club. Choose a small number of activities with enough care to enjoy repeatedly. Some days you will earn very little and have a good day anyway.

Collecting should leave something visible in your home or shared with another person. Let a funny shell become a gift, a shelf decoration, or the subject of a conversation instead of only a checklist entry.

Money should create wants

The room comes with an introductory work arrangement. After that, housing payments are predictable and forgiving: missed payments become a conversation or extra work, never eviction or loss of possessions.

Income is mainly for furniture, clothes, outings, and projects. Basic meals and a place to sleep stay within easy reach. Do not simulate hunger, bills, and exhaustion so aggressively that moving away from home feels like a punishment.

A TOWN BEFORE THE PLAYER

Give them reasons
to know each other.

Six people with relationships between them will make a better starting town than twenty people waiting separately for the player.

ADA / FERRY ENGINEER

She knows when everyone leaves.

Works early, sleeps after lunch, plays cards at the bathhouse. Her grown son has taken a mainland job; she insists the ferry timetable is all she needs to know about it. She and the baker buy each other birthday presents but claim they are only old colleagues.

Your way in: the repair yard. Later, she trusts you to help prepare the ferry for the harbor parade.

BASIL / BAKER & LANDLORD

Helpful at inconvenient times.

Leaves unsold bread at your door and knocks too early to ask whether the tap still leaks. His sister owns half the bakery and wants a different menu. He borrows tools from Ada and forgets to return them.

Your way in: your room. Over time you can set boundaries, invite him upstairs, and help with the shop without becoming his unpaid caretaker.

JUNE / CAFÉ WORKER

She wants out of here.

Grew up on the island. Saves for music school and secretly likes running the café more than she admits. Her closest friend is the observatory volunteer, whom she keeps trying to recruit into a band.

Your way in: a shift together. Her ambitions continue even if you are busy elsewhere.

OMAR / OBSERVATORY VOLUNTEER

He is much less shy after dark.

Repairs radios during the day. Organizes a badly attended astronomy club. Argues with June about music and with the harbor office about floodlights. Has a very confident opinion about biscuits.

Your way in: an open club night. Eventually you help host an evening that attracts more than the usual two people.

NELL / RETIRED SCHOOLTEACHER

She still gets the first word.

Runs the allotment and unofficially arbitrates disputes. Taught Basil, Ada, and half the port. Is learning to swim and does not want her former pupils to find out she is afraid of the water.

Your way in: a borrowed planting pot. She may ask you for help precisely because you did not grow up here.

PIP / YOUR NEXT-DOOR NEIGHBOR

Also pretending to know what they are doing.

Arrived six weeks before you. Has already bought an impractical sofa. Works odd hours, forgets dinner, and knows the best place to watch the ferry lights. Often becomes your friend before anyone more established does.

Your way in: helping get the sofa around the landing. Later, shared meals and visits with no favor attached.

How relationships grow

Shared time, remembered preferences, invitations, and showing up when you said you would. Gifts are one small part. People also disagree, cancel plans, introduce you to friends, and do things for you.

Let friendship change how daily life works: borrowing a tool, having a regular seat, being greeted from across the street. Keep a private progression model if necessary; avoid displaying every friendship as a resource to maximize.

What happens without you

Ada and Basil still meet for cards. June still rehearses. Nell still takes swimming lessons. Sometimes you hear about an event afterward; sometimes your participation changes it. Important opportunities return in another form.

Author a manageable set of recurring routines and seasonal changes. We do not need to simulate every minute to make these lives believable. We do need conversations and scenes that acknowledge what those people did.

WHAT BRINGS YOU BACK

Tuesday has its own appeal.

You still have to check the street names.

Work a short delivery round. Spend part of the pay on a blue lamp you saw in the secondhand shop. Get lost looking for the bathhouse and find the hill path instead. Eat dinner at the café because your room has no table yet. Pip knocks that evening to borrow a screwdriver.

The reward is small but concrete: you know another route, own something you chose, and have met your neighbor.

The first week

Learn the town at walking speed. Try a job. Make the room comfortable. Meet the recurring cast through ordinary encounters. The first weekend market gathers familiar faces in one place and gives you an affordable thing to save for.

The first season

Keep a job or try another. Get invited somewhere. Develop a hobby. Host someone in your room. Take part in a shared project, such as reopening the bathhouse garden or preparing the harbor parade.

The project is something the neighbors already wanted. You join a committee, contribute, and see others contribute too. They do not stand still until you collect all the materials.

The first year

The town changes with the seasons and with its people’s plans. June auditions. Pip considers a new apartment. Ada takes a holiday and discovers the ferry can survive without her. Some plans succeed; others change.

The anniversary of your arrival provides a quiet milestone. You host a gathering in a place that now feels like yours. The game can acknowledge what you have built without declaring that your life is complete.

Afterward

Continue living there. Keep meaningful recurring events, visitors, home projects, and changing ordinary dialogue. Promise a carefully authored first year and a pleasant continuing town, not infinite bespoke stories.

A calendar that fits the player’s life

Use an in-game clock with days that advance while playing and a clear “finish the day” option at home. Let players pause and save anywhere safe. Returning after two weeks away should feel welcoming. Nobody lost their home or stopped being your friend because you had other things to do.

Keep seasons long enough to establish routines, and allow scheduled appointments to offer convenient time slots. Test day length rather than committing to it before the daily activities exist.

Why this holds together

Work pays for your home. Work also introduces people. Your home gives you somewhere to invite them. Friends lead you to activities and places. Seasons change what you look forward to. All of those systems serve the same life: becoming independent and belonging somewhere.

What makes it its own game

The working port, the upstairs-room beginning, the freedom to try modest jobs, and friendships across generations give it a specific social setting. The ferry keeps a larger world present. Your old life still exists: family can write, visit, and discover the life you have made.

This is familiar life-sim territory. Its distinction has to come from the town, its people, and the texture of daily living. A clever pitch alone will not establish that.

02 / A DIFFERENT LIFE: MAKING A HOUSEHOLD

The House on
Linden Street

You inherit a large house.
It already comes with people.

The situation

Your aunt has retired to somewhere warmer and left you her old boarding house in a small hill town. There are five long-term tenants, a spare room, a leaky conservatory, and a kitchen with rules nobody has written down.

You move into the smallest bedroom. The tenants know the house far better than you do. They have jobs, friends, and routines in the surrounding town. Several have lived here long enough to remember your childhood visits.

The life you build

Make the house comfortable while deciding what kind of household you want. Cook a communal dinner or keep to yourself. Fix a room, choose a new tenant, plant the garden, make space for a workshop. Go into town for work, supplies, friendships, and your own interests.

Residents pay a predictable share and help in ways that suit them. The player is a housemate and steward. Keep enough money decisions to make improvements matter, with no hotel-style occupancy targets.

The people and the ongoing story

A schoolteacher marks papers at the kitchen table. A night-shift nurse needs quiet. A divorced carpenter has his daughter on alternate weekends. A retired dancer has claimed the conservatory. A new tenant does not yet know where anything goes.

The aunt’s old arrangements no longer suit everyone. Over a year, the household decides what to keep and what to change. A tenant may move in with a partner; another may return after a bad attempt at living alone. You also have to decide how much of your own life to give this place.

An ordinary evening

Buy a secondhand table, bring it home with the carpenter, and discover it blocks the kitchen door. Rearrange the room. Make soup. Two residents join you; one takes a bowl upstairs. Later, the nurse leaves a note asking you to save her some for the morning.

The payoff is a home with other people in it, including moments when they are inconvenient.

Why choose it?

You want a close cast, domestic detail, shared meals, rooms full of history, and relationships that develop through living together.

What sustains it?

House improvements, your own work and friendships, changing tenants, family visits, shared traditions, and seeing people use rooms you made.

What could spoil it?

Turning residents into satisfaction meters, making the player everyone’s servant, or spending the whole game indoors. Household reciprocity and the surrounding town are essential.

Production fit: strong use for furniture, interiors, the cast, dialogue, and scenes. Needs much richer domestic animation, room behavior, cooking, and visitor routines. Smaller geography does not mean inexpensive character work.

03 / A DIFFERENT LIFE: LEAVING WITH PEOPLE

The Long Road

Your home travels.
Your neighbors come with you.

The situation

You join a small traveling fair for its yearly circuit. Your new home is a worn caravan between a cook’s wagon and the puppet theater. The others have traveled together for years: they have preferred campsites, old debts, recurring arguments, and people waiting for them in each town.

You are the new hand. You help set up, learn a trade, and gradually become someone the others rely on. There is no secret reason you were chosen. You wanted to see somewhere else.

The life you build

Decorate your caravan, choose work at the fair, explore each stop, and get to know both your traveling neighbors and the people you revisit along the route. Earn money for your home and craft. Share meals after closing. Decide how to spend the last free afternoon before moving on.

The fair stays at each place for several in-game days. Travel is a communal transition with a packing day, a journey, and an arrival, rather than an open-world driving game.

The people and the ongoing story

The cook is teaching her nephew. The puppeteer is making a new show and refusing to admit the old one has become stale. The mechanic has a partner in one of the towns. The owner is considering retirement, and nobody agrees on what the fair should become.

The first circuit ends at a major autumn fair. By then, you have made a place for yourself and helped choose what this group will carry into another year. Your caravan is a record of places, work, and people.

An ordinary day

Help open the puppet theater. Spend the afternoon in a seaside town looking for fabric and a swimming spot. Bring someone from the fair along. Return for dinner, hang a new curtain in your caravan, and hear the town band practicing beyond the camp.

Next season you return to that same stop. Someone remembers you.

Why choose it?

You want travel and discovery without giving up a familiar home or recurring friends. A shared journey gives the cast a reason to stay together.

What sustains it?

Your caravan, learning a craft, relationships within the fair, revisiting towns, seasonal preparations, and the anticipation of the next stop.

What could spoil it?

Too many shallow destinations, repetitive setup chores, or timers that punish exploration. Begin with three substantial stops and a small traveling company.

Production fit: reuses modular world tools, interiors, furniture, schedules, and music. Requires multiple destinations, persistent state per stop, travel transitions, and more environment content. This is the largest reconstruction of the three.

WHICH GAME SHOULD WE MAKE?

I would choose
Little Harbor.

Its central promise is easy to want: leave home, find a little place, and make a life among people you come to know.

The existing island can become somewhere to live rather than a backdrop for a story sequence. The ferry, small cast, furniture, weather, and music all have natural jobs. The postal role can survive as one part-time occupation if it earns its place.

More importantly, it supports different kinds of players in the same world. One cares about their room. Another likes a routine. Another comes back for the neighbors. Those interests reinforce each other.

The real choice

Little Harbor: “I want a place of my own, and a town that gets to know me.”

Linden Street: “I want to make a home with a complicated little household.”

The Long Road: “I want to see the world with people who become my family.”

Each can contain charming activities. The life is the reason those activities belong together.

The first version worth building

A single inhabited block

Your room above the bakery, a café, the dock, Pip’s room, and one quiet outdoor spot. Four residents with routines and relationships. The rest of town can wait.

Three playable days

Arrive and unpack. Try a short shift. Buy and place one thing you wanted. Meet the same person in two different contexts. Have someone visit your room. See a conversation between two residents that is not about you.

One small anticipation

A shared dinner on the third evening. Let the player prepare, attend, arrive late, or hear about it afterward. Invitations should make time feel meaningful without making the schedule threatening.

The question to test

After those three days, ask: “What would you like to do tomorrow?” Listen for a particular person, place, purchase, or plan. If the answer is only “whatever the next quest says,” the town is not working yet.

What to discard

The afterlife explanation, resident-resolution arcs, the compulsory mail spine, and the premise that the player must restore everybody. Reconsider the title and all biographies. Do not preserve old plot logic because it is already wired up.

What to carry forward

The authored island pipeline, useful character and animation work, interaction foundations, furniture placement, sound and weather, scene tools, and save infrastructure. Inspect each for the new game’s needs. The current systems do not yet add up to this simulation.
