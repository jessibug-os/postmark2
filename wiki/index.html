    POSTMARK is a cozy post-human town-builder. You wake in a forest with no memories and one address — POSTMASTER, FRONTIER TOWN — and find a valley whose people vanished forty years ago and whose clockwork townsfolk simply ran out of light and stopped. You reactivate them one at a time, build them somewhere to work, and grow an empty plot of ground back into a town that knows its own name.

    It is Animal Crossing's daily rhythm with a mystery underneath it: every villager you befriend gives up one piece of their memory of the morning everyone left, and a sack of undelivered mail in the post office holds the rest. See The Dead Letter Office.

    New here? Read Getting started for the first hour, then Controls. If you are hunting a specific fish or bug, the fish and bug tables are filterable.

    The core loop

    1. Explore & scavenge — dig ★ spots, fell dead trees, fish, catch bugs, forage.
    2. Reactivate a robot — an Amber Cell wakes a dormant unit.
    3. Build them somewhere to be — timber and stone raise their shop, archive or cottage.
    4. They move in — and bring a service, a schedule, and a piece of the story.
    5. The map opens — a new zone unlocks. Repeat.

    Getting started

    Your first crossing to St. Mystere, in order.

    This guide covers the live Kindly Forward opening. The rest of this wiki still contains legacy POSTMARK reference material while the runtime migration is completed.

    The intro

    You begin aboard an ordinary regional ferry on your first Postmaster assignment. A fellow recruit asks your name and destination, then recognizes St. Mystere as an office decommissioned years ago. The passengers leave at successive stops, fog closes around the boat, and you wake at an empty berth with one voice calling inland. Albert, the island's frightened administrative clerk, explains the stopped ferries and asks you to help reopen the post office. Press Esc or choose Skip opening on a repeat start to enter the same first objective without replaying the crossing.

    Day one checklist

    1. Find Albert at the post office. The opening keeps him as the same live resident you met during the reveal rather than replacing him after the cutscene.
    2. Repair the arrival board and recover the missing drawer key. This is the first live Kindly Forward objective after the crossing.
    3. Begin reopening the office. St. Mystere starts empty because the player is rebuilding the conditions in which stranded residents can arrive and stay.

    Early habits worth forming

    • Read the morning postmark. On the first visit each real day it names one resident request and delivers a small parcel to your pockets. Bring that exact item through Give a gift for a generous friendship bonus. A new request replaces it tomorrow, so missing a day creates no debt.
    • Talk to everyone once a day. The first conversation each real day is worth 8 friendship points with that villager, free. See Friendship & hearts.
    • Dig every ★ you see. They reset daily and they are the only source of Cut Stone, the main source of Amber Cells, and the only source of relics.
    • Donate the first of everything to CATALOGER 734 before you sell it. He only wants one of each, and a donated species is gone from your pockets but recorded forever.
    • Sell the rest to REGGIE. Scrap metal and duplicate fish are the early economy.
    • Check the mailbox each morning once you have a heart with anyone — villagers write to you, sometimes with gifts.

    Common early questions

    Where are the ★ dig spots?
    Five are active at a time, scattered anywhere in the zones you have unlocked, and they reshuffle at the start of each real day. Unlocking the forest and the meadow widens the pool but does not increase the count.
    I chopped a tree and got nothing.
    A dead tree takes three swings to come down (two with the Felling Axe). Living trees can be shaken for apples but never felled.
    The fish keeps getting away.
    The strike window is 0.75 seconds from the moment the bobber dips. Strike on the dip, not the wobble — BUOY will tell you the same thing, less politely.
    Nothing is biting / nothing is in the grass.
    Almost every species is gated on season, hour, or place. Check the fish and bug tables — the pond at midnight holds things the river at noon never will.

    Villagers

    Eight clockwork residents. Every one of them was here before you and remembers something you don't.

    The resident roster is fixed. Nobody is evicted or randomly replaced: all eight are witnesses in the same mystery, and their homes, letters, routines and heart scenes remain part of your town. Festivals and visiting performers may bring guests without displacing a resident.

    All eight villagers start dormant — powered down where they stood when their charge ran out. Each needs one Amber Cell to reactivate. Five are part of the main quest chain; three (WICK, FERN and PLINK) are wanderer units you can find and wake in any order once you have a spare cell.

    Friendship & hearts

    How the numbers under the hearts actually work.

    Hearts

    Max5 ♥ per villager
    Points per heart50
    Point cap250
    Daily talk+8 (first talk each real day)
    Loved gift+25
    Liked gift+12
    Neutral gift+6
    Disliked gift+2
    Favor errand+20 on top of the gift value
    Gift limitOne counted gift per villager per day
    Scenes at1 ♥ · 3 ♥ · 5 ♥
    Tracked inTown Ledger — L

    Friendship is stored as points, one heart per 50, capped at five hearts (250 points). There are three ways to earn them, and they stack.

    1. Talk to them, once a day

    The first conversation with each villager each real day is worth +8. Subsequent conversations that day are free but worth nothing. Talking to all eight every day is 64 points — more than a heart — for about a minute of walking.

    Each villager has at least 150 individually written everyday lines. What they choose depends on the hour, weekday, season, weather, location, story progress, friendship, festivals and birthdays. They also notice the last substantial thing you actually did: after fishing, catching a bug, digging, chopping, gifting, donating or building, the next villager you speak to acknowledges it once before their normal conversation rotation resumes.

    2. Give a gift they like

    Only one gift per villager per day counts (a favor item is the exception; see below). Tastes are discoverable in conversation, and the reaction tells you which bracket you hit.

    VerdictPointsNotes
    Loved+25Two or three specific items per villager. See each villager's page.
    Liked+12Some villagers like whole categories — CATALOGER 734 likes every fish and every bug; MARIGOLD and FERN like all forage; PLINK likes all bugs; BUOY likes all fish.
    Neutral+6Anything not on their list. Never a wasted gift.
    Disliked+2Still positive. They are robots and they are lonely.
    Efficient route: CATALOGER 734 likes every catchable species, so the surplus of a fishing session raises him fastest. MARIGOLD and FERN both like all forage, so one afternoon of picking feeds two villagers.

    3. Favor errands

    Villagers occasionally ask you for a specific item. Handing it over is worth its normal gift value +20, and it ignores the one-gift-per-day limit.

    What hearts unlock

    • 1 ♥ — first heart moment; the villager starts writing you letters.
    • 3 ♥ — second heart moment; the higher tier of mailbox letters, which carry gifts.
    • 5 ♥ — third heart moment, a unique keepsake that cannot be sold, permanently warmer dialogue, and the Kindred Spirits achievement.

    Total town hearts also gate the final act of the main story.

    Design note — Gifts never go negative and the daily talk is generous, because the fantasy is "the town is glad you came", not "manage eight relationship meters". The one-gift-a-day cap exists purely so you can't buy a friendship with a full inventory in ninety seconds.

    Six quests from waking up in the forest to the Founding Ceremony. Mild spoilers for the build-up; none for the ending.

    The Dead Letter Office

    The main story. Everything below the first section is a spoiler and is collapsed by default.

    ⚠ Heavy spoilers. This article documents the ending of the game. Each act is behind a click. There is a Reveal spoilers switch in the top bar if you would rather just read it all.

    The Dead Letter Office

    TypeMain story arc
    Starts afterThe Founding Ceremony
    Letters15, across 3 acts (5 / 6 / 4)
    Delivered toVillagers and landmarks

    What it is (no spoilers)

    The post office holds a mail sack that never went out — letters written the week the town emptied, addressed to people and places that stopped existing before the postmaster could deliver them. You can now deliver them.

    Each letter has an address. Carrying it to the right villager or the right landmark — the well, the dock, the bridge, the orchard, the Archive terrace — plays a staged, letterboxed scene and adds one truth fragment to your Case File. Three acts. The third is gated behind the town's total hearts, so the ending arrives when you have actually made friends, not when you have walked far enough.

    Design note — The story is delivered by the same verb as the job: you find out what happened by doing your job. No journal to read, no lore notes on the ground. The postmaster learns the truth by delivering the mail, forty years late.

    Act I — What happened

    Spoiler: Act I

    The town received a Notice of Resettlement. A mining concern claimed the valley's ore rights and everyone was moved to a work-city called Cinderhollow — "temporarily," pending the township's appeal. Wagons were provided. Residents were told to bring only what they could carry, and that the valley would be held in trust and returned.

    Everyone read the word temporary and packed a single trunk. They said "by spring," because the letter said so.

    How fishing works

    Shadows drift in the water. Cast near one with F; the nearest shadow takes interest, the bobber dips, and you have 0.75 seconds to press F again. Shadow size (small, medium, large) tells you roughly what class of fish is under it — the 2,600-gear Depot Sturgeon never hides under a small shadow.

    The pond is the slow, wide stretch of the same river spline, and the game tells it apart by channel width rather than treating it as a separate lake. If a species says "pond," fish the broad water; if it says "river," fish the narrow runs.

    Night

    Night is not a blue filter. The world goes deep blue, WICK's lamps throw painted pools of warm light onto the ground, and every occupied building lights its windows from the inside. It is also where the most valuable fish and bugs live — four fish and six bugs are night-only, including the three priciest catches in the game.

    Solar power

    Villagers run on solar. At night they switch to low-power mode and sleep where they stand — you can still talk to them, and they will tell you they were asleep. Two villagers run the schedule inverted: CATALOGER 734 and WICK are awake 8 PM to about 4 AM and dormant through the day.

    Weather

    Rain, snow, fog, and seasonal falling petals and leaves.

    Weather

    RainWater ripples, surface-specific impacts, wet ground, puddles and roof runoff
    SnowWinter only; lays over the whole map
    FogDraws the draw distance in close
    Petals / leavesSeasonal ambient fall
    AffectsDialogue selection · ambience layers

    Weather is atmospheric rather than punitive — nothing is gated behind it and no activity is blocked by it. What it does change is what the villagers say: dialogue is picked using time of day, weekday, location, weather, friendship and story progress together, so a rainy Tuesday afternoon on the dock produces lines you will not hear on a clear Sunday morning in the square. Recent fishing, catching, digging, chopping, gifting, donating and building can also prompt a one-time acknowledgement from the next villager you speak to.

    Rain belongs to the surface it reaches. Water opens into rings; sand, soil and grass darken; hard roofs and timber answer with a sharper patter; tree crowns catch drops before they reach the ground. Sustained rain fills deliberately placed hollows and starts a few strong eave streams. Every gameplay cell keeps its own moisture value. Rain intensity, soil, slope and cover decide how quickly it changes: sand drains, dirt holds water, low flats pool, slopes shed moisture downhill, and level wet cells pass moisture toward drier outlets after a storm. Tree or roof shade slows both soaking and drying. When the cloud passes, the streaks stop first while wet ground, sheltered soil and puddles dry on different schedules.

    "There's a piece I only play when it rains. I'll not tell you which. You'll know it when it comes." — PLINK

    The soundtrack reacts too: weather adds ambience layers over the day/dawn/dusk/night themes, which crossfade rather than cutting. See Tech & rendering.

    Design doc

    The intentions behind the systems. This page doubles as the project's living design document — if a rule here and the game disagree, one of them needs changing.

    Pillars

    ToneCozy first, with an undercurrent of melancholy and wonder
    ReferenceAnimal Crossing, heavily — in every aspect
    AmbitionWhat Stardew did for Harvest Moon: same cozy surface, deeper heart
    Tech era19th century, clockwork
    Player fantasyThe last human, made welcome
    Anti-goalChores. Nothing in the game punishes a day off.

    Premise

    Post-apocalyptic cozy life-sim. The player is the last human, arriving as postmaster-turned-mayor of an abandoned settlement. The town starts empty. She wakes in the forest, stumbles on a deactivated administrative robot, and rebuilds a frontier hamlet into a real town one resident at a time.

    The core loop, and why it is that shape

    1. Explore & scavenge — the world is the resource.
    2. Restore a robot — one Amber Cell, one person back.
    3. Build them somewhere to function — a robot without a building has a job it can't do.
    4. They move in and add a service — the town gets a verb it did not have.
    5. The map unlocks further — new ground, new species, new dormant units.

    Each turn of the loop must add a verb, not just a number. REGGIE adds buying and selling. CATALOGER adds collecting-as-record. MARIGOLD, BUOY, WICK, FERN and PLINK add company, which is the actual point.

    Rules the systems are held to

    • No species is available everywhere, always. Every fish and bug is gated on at least one of season, hour or place — a species you can catch anywhere at any time teaches the player nothing about the world.
    • Gifts never go negative. Even a disliked gift is +2. The town is glad you came.
    • Show, don't tell. Items pop out of trees and holes as physical icons that arc, bounce, rest, then fly to your pockets. Inventory changes at the moment of collection.
    • The story is delivered by the job. You learn what happened by delivering mail, not by reading a lore note on the ground.
    • Every villager needs four things — a job, a schedule, an idle quirk, and a gait. If a character can't fill all four, they aren't ready to exist.
    • Nothing punishes absence. No crops die, no friendship decays to zero, no timer runs out while you are away.

    Art direction

    Concept art reference is painterly gouache storybook: aged brass, ivory, ink-black, a muted earthy palette, and exactly one warm amber glow per character.

    The in-engine target is deliberately different. Low-poly retro-3D, roughly 600–900 triangles per character. Detail lives in textures, not geometry: 128–256 px hand-painted texture sheets with the lighting baked in. Faces are painted sheets that swap live between idle, blink, happy, surprised and asleep.

    Camera is Animal Crossing-style: elevated, angled down, fixed-ish, following the player.

    Kindly Forward pipeline status (2026-07-30): ten rigged GLBs now load through the authored asset registry with baked 128px sheets and idle/walk clips. Later modelling passes add distinct faces, jaw/chin head planes, tailored torso profiles, posed two-part arms, clothing layers, footwear and signature props. The meshes remain replaceable production scaffolds and all visual quality remains awaiting human review.

    Design note — The gap between the concept art and the engine look is intentional. The gouache boards set the palette and mood; the low-poly build delivers them through painted texture sheets and a low-res render target. Concept art that could be reproduced literally in engine would not have been ambitious enough about colour.

    The retro pipeline

    The scene renders to a low-resolution WebGLRenderTarget at about 480 × 360 and is then blitted up with nearest-neighbour filtering — so the whole world is genuinely low-res rather than a post-process filter over a high-res image. A vertex-snapping step in the vertex shader produces the PSX-era wobble. The final blit carries the film grade.

    Materials are MeshLambertMaterial with flat shading. There is no PBR and there are no realtime shadows; every character, tree, prop and building footprint gets a blob shadow instead, so nothing floats.

    Ground

    The map texture carries biomes, worn dirt paths with trodden edges, cliff strata and riverbed cobbles. A separate tiling detail sheet adds blade-level grass patterning and wind ripples in the sand on top of it — a second material pass rather than more pixels in the base map.

    Characters

    Every model is procedural low-poly geometry built in code, ~600–900 triangles, wearing a canvas-painted texture sheet. Faces are painted sheets that swap live between expressions. Animation is hand-authored gait: footfall squash, turn lean, bent elbows, spring on hair and satchel — and each villager has their own gait parameters, so SPARKS bustles, BUOY rolls and PLINK bounces on the beat.

    Audio

    Everything is synthesized at runtime. Each of the 24 real-clock hours has its own authored melody built around the six-note POSTMARK hook; spring, summer, autumn and winter reharmonize it, and interiors soften the arrangement. Shop and ceremony themes crossfade in as events; weather adds ambience layers over the top; and birdsong, crickets and river wash sit underneath. Each villager has an Animalese-style pitched voice — SPARKS is a bright 620 Hz square wave, REGGIE a low 300 Hz sawtooth.

    PLINK's concert set: eight 900-gear records rotate through the Emporium once it reaches its second tier. Collected records appear on the gramophone in your cottage: approach it, press E, and choose a side. The set runs from Postmark Overture to Homecoming, With Blank.

    Interface

    All UI is a DOM overlay above the canvas, so text stays crisp while the 3D scene stays deliberately pixelated. The look is postal: kraft-paper dialogue cards with per-character portrait medallions and nameplate colours, wax-seal key prompts, and a display serif throughout.

    What this is

    A player-facing reference for POSTMARK that doubles as the project's living design document. Two audiences, one page set: someone learning to play can find the fish table and the controls, and someone building the game can find the intent behind a system next to its actual numbers.

    Every price, drop rate, hour window and season on this wiki was read out of the game's own data tables — the item catalogue, the fish and bug definitions, the crafting recipes, the calendar, the friendship rules and the villager schedules. If a number here disagrees with the game, the game is right and this page needs an edit.

    • Start here — the first hour and the controls.

    • Town & economy — tools, crafting, the shop, your house.
