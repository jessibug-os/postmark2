Target spec — an acre grid, not a height field

St. Mystere is assembled from authored square blocks called acres, laid on a fixed grid so that neighbouring pieces agree by construction. Nothing is carved out of noise. Replayability comes from which acres are chosen and how they are arranged, and every arrangement the solver can reach is playable.

Why this replaces the previous contract. The first pass generated a sine-based coastline, a hardcoded dock coordinate, a monotone river spline and three fixed terrace circles — four independent coordinate systems that the code then tried to reconcile after the fact. Measured on 2026-07-29: 3 of 5 seeds put the dock in open ocean, 5 of 5 ran the river mouth past the coastline, and 4 of 5 placed the summit at beach height (one of those in the sea). Those were not four bugs. They were one: continuous geometry offers no guarantee that two separately-computed features agree. On an acre grid each of those states is unrepresentable rather than merely fixed.

Grid

  • Acre = 14 × 14 world units. Big enough to hold a house and its yard, small enough that the river can turn inside the island.
  • Each acre contains a 16 × 16 gameplay grid. A cell is 0.875 world units. Trees, rocks, holes, buried items and outdoor furniture occupy stable cells; meshes may vary visually inside them.
  • Grid = 16 × 12 acres = 224 × 168 units. Raised from 12 × 10 after the tighter grid left no room for a plateau, a pond and a varied coast at once.
  • The outer ring is always ocean. That guarantees water on all four sides without a single check, leaving a 14 × 10 interior of 140 placeable acres.
  • Land occupies 62–77 of those 140 — roughly the 70% figure the earlier draft asked for, now counted in whole acres.

Authoring cost scales with the library, not the map

  • The grid holds 120 acres; the library is about 18 authored piece types, each with 2–3 visual variants. Four rotations per piece are free.
  • That is ~40–50 meshes total, and it is the same class of task as character production: bounded, authored, human-reviewed.
  • Every piece is hand-made. This satisfies AGENTS.md rule 4 — procedural chooses arrangement, never the asset. The previous sine coastline was procedural art standing in for design, the exact failure that rule names.

Start state

One ruined post office, one arrival dock, and a short walk between them. No resident homes, no service buildings, no bridges, no village clutter. Natural ground is quiet rather than barren: grass, exposed rock, beach, wind-bent shrubs, a few starter resources. No pre-placed home plots — the first housing quest teaches the player to survey and choose. The generator supplies validity; the player supplies intent.

Open tunable: 168 × 140 is 5.7× the playable area of an Animal Crossing town, which is deliberately small enough to know by heart. If early walkthroughs feel thin rather than generous, shrink the grid to 10 × 8 acres (140 × 112) before adding content to fill it. Acre size is the one number to change; everything downstream is expressed in acres.

An acre is a transition, not a state

Revision of 2026-08-13. The acre-tileset model above is retained in full; what changes is where a terrain boundary lives. It moves off the acre border and into the acre interior, which is the single decision that separates our silhouette from Animal Crossing's.

The defect this fixes. Under the model as first written, every acre is uniformly one kind at one tier, so every boundary between two kinds is an acre border — a straight 14-unit line. Shorelines, cliff edges and river banks all came out as axis-aligned segments joined at right angles, which reads as a stencil rather than as a coast. This is not a shading problem and no amount of downstream noise fixes it: the straightness follows from where we decided the feature lives.

What the reference actually shows

Read against the Animal Crossing: New Leaf acre set (~180 authored terrain acres):

  • Boundaries run through acre interiors. A coast acre stacks grass, sand and water with both transitions in its middle band. No acre is uniformly one thing.
  • Therefore an edge is not one terrain. A single edge reads grass to ~0.55, sand ~0.55–0.70, water below. The edge is a profile, not a label.
  • Crossing offsets are quantised. That is the only reason ~180 hand-drawn acres tile at all.
  • Coast is three offset curves — cliff top, sand line, waterline — wobbling semi-independently, not one boundary drawn three times.
  • Rivers meander in the centreline at near-constant width, not the reverse.
  • Corner radii are large, roughly a quarter-acre sweep. Timid rounding reads as a bevelled box, which is worse than a sharp corner.
  • Collision stays per-cell and blocky while the art curves. Silhouette and gameplay lattice are already decoupled there.

The three changes

  • C1 — the acre is a transition. A boundary passes through it; the border is where two boundary segments hand off, not where state changes.
  • C2 — the socket is a segmented edge profile. An ordered list of spans covering [0,1], each naming a surface. socketsCompatible becomes span-list equality with one side mirrored. Offsets are quantised to sixteenths, matching the 16 × 16 gameplay grid, so a crossing always lands on a cell boundary and the socket space stays finite.
  • C3 — silhouette derives from feature curves; the gameplay lattice does not. The solver emits a coastline loop, a river centreline, cliff polylines and pond rings, fitted through the socket crossing points. Everything visual reads a signed distance to those curves. IslandCellGrid keeps classifying by sampling at the cell centre, so a cell is wholly one thing and placement determinism is untouched.

The objection, and its answer

Interior boundaries were tried once and abandoned, correctly, for this reason: an acre whose western half sat a tier down still presented LAND@its own tier to its northern and southern neighbours across the whole shared border, so the socket was a lie about half of every one of those edges.

That reasoning is sound given a socket that carries one label. The socket type was the defect, not the conclusion. A segmented profile can say "grass for the first nine sixteenths, then sand" — which is exactly the statement an interior boundary needs to make, and it makes the socket and the surface the same statement again.

The noise budget

Displacement is allocated by scale and may not cross between bands. Acre scale (14u) belongs to topology — the solver alone. Feature scale (2–6u) is curve displacement. Sub-unit is texture and props. No octave may straddle the 0.875-unit cell size, or a cell stops being unambiguously one thing and placement goes mushy.

What the contract loses

Several clauses exist only to keep border-located boundaries representable by a finite square-piece vocabulary. Curves have no vocabulary, so they retire rather than being satisfied:

  • The one-ocean-edge rule retires. A curve entering the north edge and leaving the south is an ordinary acre, not an isthmus to prune. The "square headland reads as a staircase" problem was an artifact of border-located boundaries, and shore-corner stops being a distinct diagonal piece — a corner is just a curve entering one edge and leaving an adjacent one.
  • Corner-pair adjacency, one-acre bays, diagonal-only joins and unsupported terrain junctions retire for the same reason.
  • Ramps stop being special. The sloped corridor with cliff shoulders was the one place the model already admitted an acre needs interior structure. It becomes an ordinary transition.

Everything else — reachability, ramp counts, river monotonicity, compactness, anchor legality — is unchanged. Net effect is a smaller gate and a wider accepted island space, so fewer resamples and more varied islands.

What replaces them — assertions on the curves

  • The coastline is a simple closed loop: no self-intersection.
  • The river is monotone in tier from spring to mouth.
  • Minimum channel width and minimum beach width hold along their whole length.
  • No cliff polyline crosses a ramp corridor.
  • Full reachability on the cell grid — unchanged in meaning, recomputed from the field.

Seam continuity stops being policed at borders and becomes true by construction, because a curve is global. This is the same argument that already made the coast distance field a shared field rather than a per-acre one.

Acceptance test. Better than "looks less blocky", and adjudicable by a jury: the curve layer must be able to reproduce any acre on the reference sheet. Twelve acres spanning the coast, river, corner and river-mouth sets; hand-specify the edge crossing offsets; assert the fitter produces that class of curve. Authored pieces remain possible throughout — a hand-drawn cove or a signature headland becomes a local modifier blended into the field, so the art budget is preserved. What changes is that we are no longer required to author all 180.

Generation contract

Nine passes, run in a fixed order. Each pass narrows what later passes may do, so a valid island is the only possible output rather than the lucky one. The seed selects choices within the constraints; it never relaxes them.

1 · Frame

Mark the entire outer ring of acres OCEAN. Ocean on every side is now structural, not a rule to be checked.

2 · Landmass

Grow from two or three separated centres until the land covers 44–52 acres, then let the lobes meet. Each lobe grows as an ellipse on a per-seed axis, so some islands run wide, some tall, some on a diagonal. A single blob grown from the middle produced the same rounded rectangle every seed — measured at 5% minimum silhouette difference across 780 pairs — because the randomness only jittered its edge. Land may never occupy the outer ring, so a beach always separates it from open water.

3 · Tiers

Every land acre adjacent to ocean becomes tier 0, the coastal shelf. Grow tier 1 inward from the shelf. Place tier 2 as one or two contiguous clusters in the interior, weighted north. Each tier region must be contiguous, and tier 2 may never touch tier 0 directly.

4 · Arrival

Choose the dock from the island's south-facing shore acres — south is fixed, which shore acre is not. Choose the post office from tier 0 or 1 land acres within three acres of the dock. Both are acres, so neither can land in water.

5 · River

Route in two legs: from a source inside the plateau across the high ground to a chosen falls on the plateau edge, then down to a mouth that is a shore acre. The falls is a landmark, so where the river drops is chosen rather than wherever the walk happened to descend. The plateau run is capped at four acres and the high ground is closed below the falls — the summit is where the water comes from, not where it goes, and a channel that wanders the tops for a dozen acres reads as a canal on a mesa. The path is a free walk over the grid — it may turn, double back and cross the island at any angle — and must include at least one cascade acre where it drops a tier.

6 · Ramps

For every tier boundary, place ramp acres until each tier region has at least one ramped route to the tier below. This is a solver requirement, not decoration: an unreachable terrace is a failed island, not a quirky one.

7 · Bridges

Flag every river acre whose two lateral neighbours are land at the same tier as a bridge candidate. Filter for approach space and duplicate region access. Guarantee at least three surviving candidates reaching distinct regions.

8 · Fill

Resolve every remaining acre to a concrete library piece by socket matching, choosing among compatible variants by seed. This is the only pass where the seed touches appearance.

9 · Validate

Run the full gate below. A seed that fails is a bug in the solver, never a seed to skip.

The socket alphabet

Superseded in form by the transition model: an edge carries an ordered list of these, each covering a span of the edge, rather than exactly one. The alphabet below is unchanged; what changed is that an edge may present several of them in sequence. A piece with a uniform edge is the one-span case.

Two acres may sit side by side only if their facing edge profiles agree. This single rule is what makes the old class of bug impossible.

  • OCEAN — open water. Compatible with OCEAN and SHORE.
  • SHORE — sand meeting water. Valid only facing OCEAN.
  • LAND@t — walkable ground at tier t. Compatible only with LAND@t at the same tier, or with a cliff/ramp acre presenting LAND@t on that side.
  • RIVER@t+offset — the river crosses this edge at tier t, at a given lateral offset. Must meet a matching offset, which is what keeps the channel continuous instead of stepping.
  • CLIFF@t→t′ — an elevation change across the edge, carried by an authored cliff or ramp piece.

Hard rule: LAND@0 may never touch LAND@2. A two-tier change must pass through a piece authored to show it.

Water never climbs. A river acre may only pass to an acre at the same tier or lower. Without this the channel drops to the shelf and climbs back onto the terrace, which reads as broken water no matter how the piece is drawn.

A full land acre presents at most one ocean edge. Retired by the transition model; retained here as the record of why it existed. Two adjacent ocean edges was an outside corner needing a diagonal half-sand piece, because a square headland reads as a staircase; two opposite edges, three or four had no piece in the library and were pruned. All of that was a consequence of boundaries living on acre borders. Under interior boundaries a coast curve may enter and leave by any pair of edges, so none of these arrangements is degenerate and none is pruned. The shelf remains exactly one acre deep; a two-deep beach reads as a sandbar with a second coastline behind it.

One island, not two. Lobe centres are placed 2.5–4.5 acres apart: far enough to make headlands and bays, close enough that the lobes genuinely merge. Scattered centres produced two bulbs joined by an awkward neck with the river forced through the join. The landmass must fill at least 52% of its bounding box, asserted — a coherent island fills most of it, two bulbs do not. Measured across seeds: 68–95%.

The plateau is a landmark, not a region. Capped at 6–9 acres and asserted at ≤10, and it must fill at least 55% of its own bounding box — the same test the island gets. Grown on a depth bias alone it came out as a thin band snaking across the terrace; a summit is a mass you stand on, so growth now prefers acres the cluster already wraps and uses depth only to break ties, keeping the cliff on one flank. Measured 58–100%, mean 83%. Sized as a share of the island it grew past twenty acres and became a second map to cross rather than a summit to climb.

The island carries five landmarks beyond the river. A pond — still water on the terrace, unconnected to the river, a second fishing spot. An offshore islet — one or two acres, on every island, deliberately exempt from the coast-shape rules because a one-acre island is exactly what those rules forbid on the mainland, and unreachable until late. Guaranteed rather than likely: a destination that only appears three seeds in four is one the player cannot rely on, so it stops being a destination at all. Cove shore variants: which way a beach faces decides what it is, so the south side is wide sand for arrivals while exposed north and the flanks break into rock. The falls where the river leaves the plateau. And the arrival: the office is asserted within three acres of the dock so the opening walk stays legible.

No corner-only land. Two land acres touching at a diagonal with water on both orthogonal sides are not walkable and sit in different 4-connected regions, so a neck that looks crossable is not. Excluded by the contract, and every land acre must be reachable from the dock with all bridges built.

Where the variety actually comes from: landmass growth, tier layout, which south shore holds the dock, the river's free path, which river acres survive as bridge candidates, and per-acre variant choice. That product is combinatorially large, and unlike the previous spline — whose source was always east, whose z always ran −55 to +61, and whose only freedom was the amplitude of one sine hump — none of it can produce an invalid island.

Tiers and ramps

Elevation is a discrete integer property of an acre, not a sampled function. Three tiers, authored transitions, no noise-derived cliffs. The player can read progress by looking up.

Tier 0 · coastal shelf · y = 0.5

Beaches wrap the whole island. Dock, tide pools, drift mail and the first resource loop live here. Ocean stays visible beyond it. Always the ring adjacent to water.

Tier 1 · settlement terrace · y = 2.2

The interior rise carrying the post office, home plots and most daily life. The largest tier by area, because this is where the game is played.

Tier 2 · high interior · y = 3.9

A single plateau of 6–9 acres, grown inward from one flank so its edge reads as one long cliff. Overlooks, mist pockets, the river's source, late-game gathering, and the summit. Never adjacent to tier 0.

Rules of the tier field

  • Ground inside an ordinary acre is flat at its tier height, plus small authored relief baked into the mesh. No per-vertex noise.
  • A ramp is a structured opening in an elevation-boundary acre, not a tilt across the acre: its sloped corridor occupies 30% of the acre's width and depth, with full-height cliff shoulders around it.
  • Each elevation boundary gets a seeded ramp, with at most four ramps on an island; disconnected tier regions may force an extra one.
  • Cliff edges carry a 1.5–2 unit visual shelf and collision margin, authored into the piece.
  • The summit sits on a tier-2 acre by definition. It cannot be elsewhere, because it is a property of an acre rather than a coordinate chosen beside one.

What this deletes

  • The height function. One tierAt(acre) lookup replaces heightAt(x,z). Terrain, placement, collision, shadows and bridge decks all read the same integer.
  • The step-function cliff. The previous heightAt returned one of three constants, so band boundaries were vertical 1.9 and 3.4 unit walls with no ramp anywhere — a spec violation that shipped because nothing checked for it.
  • Reconciliation passes. The old generator shoved the post office sideways when it landed in the river and again when connectivity failed. Sockets make both cases impossible to construct.

Water, shoreline and river

The ocean is the outer ring. The river is a chain of authored acres. Both are structural, so neither can end up somewhere it should not be.

Ocean and beaches

  • The outer ring is ocean on all four sides — no invisible wall presented as land.
  • Shore acres carry a 6–10 unit beach, widening at the dock and narrowing into rocky coves as authored variants.
  • Beach collision permits walking, fishing and tide gathering while blocking departure.
  • Swimming and diving are ocean-only and can never bypass a river gate — a river acre's banks are solid.

River as a chain

  • Source is an acre on the tier-2 boundary. Mouth is a shore acre — so the river ends at the coast by construction. The previous spline ran to a fixed z = 61 and overshot into open sea on every seed.
  • Piece types: straight, bend, cascade (tier drop), basin (pond widening), spring (source), mouth.
  • Channel width is a property of the piece, widening toward the mouth; one seed-chosen basin acre gives the pond.
  • Every water query — collision, fishing, foam, bridge validity, minimap — reads the same acre chain.
The variety gate. The old river's topology was fixed: always east source, always north-to-south, one sine hump. It passed its gate because the gate only tested reproducibility. The new gate tests both directions — the same seed must reproduce the same acre grid, and different seeds must differ by at least 25% of their acre assignments, with the river path differing in shape and not merely in offset. Sameness is now a test failure.

Traversal and bridge gates

Water paces the game. Each crossing is previewed, approached safely, and unlocked by a visible civic project the player chooses the site for.

Candidates fall out of the grid

A bridge candidate is simply a river acre whose two lateral neighbours are land at the same tier. No geometric search, no tuning: the condition is a socket comparison. Candidates are then filtered for approach space and for adding genuinely new region access, and the survivors are shown as survey stakes for the player to choose between.

Bridge 1 · early

The first quest reveals several candidates. The player picks the crossing that best serves their first home and resource route. Costs a timber-and-rope kit. Opens one new region.

Bridge 2 · midgame

A later survey reveals a new candidate set. Requires stone footings and a delivered letter that reveals the route. Opens the high interior or another seed-dependent district, and changes the skyline.

Bridge 3 · late

The final set connects the settled island to the dead ferry landing. Requires restored records and a community contribution. No fixed world coordinate at any stage.

Collision and navigation

  • River banks are solid; the player cannot wade. Bridge decks meet their banks at the same tier, which sockets guarantee rather than a check.
  • Each candidate has a deck preview, two ramp previews and a construction-state collider.
  • Once built, the chosen candidate enables a nav-graph edge between acres and updates camera framing, footstep material and resident schedules.

Implementation and the gate

The solver is smaller than the generator it replaces, and its gate tests the things that actually broke.

Modules

  • AcreLibrary.ts — piece types, their edge sockets, variants, and the compatibility table.
  • FeatureCurves.ts — the coastline loop, river centreline, cliff polylines and pond rings, fitted through socket crossing points. The only module that decides silhouette.
  • IslandSolver.ts — the nine passes; emits an acre grid.
  • IslandGrid.ts — the grid itself plus acreAt, tierAt, socketBetween queries.
  • IslandCellGrid.ts — the seamless 256 × 192 placement grid, terrain capabilities, footprints and layered occupancy.
  • IslandMesh.ts — instantiates library meshes per acre; the only module that touches Three.js.
  • IslandTraversal.ts — bridge candidates, construction state, nav-graph edges. Salvageable from the current pass.
  • PlotPlacement.ts — player survey mode over acres. Salvageable.
  • IslandScatter.ts — per-acre resource quotas. Salvageable.

The gate — what island-check.mjs must assert

  • Zero socket mismatches across all 120 acres.
  • Dock is a south-facing shore acre. Post office is a land acre, tier ≤ 1, reachable from the dock with every bridge closed.
  • Summit is on a tier-2 acre.
  • Every tier region has ≥ 1 ramp to the tier below.
  • River source is on the tier-2 boundary; river mouth is a shore acre; ≥ 1 cascade acre.
  • ≥ 3 bridge candidates reaching distinct regions.
  • Flood-fill: each bridge choice adds reachable ground without stranding the office, plots or quest anchors.
  • Reproducibility — same seed, same grid.
  • Dissimilarity — different seeds differ by ≥ 25% of acre assignments.
Why the last two matter most. The previous gate asserted bounds, a bridge count, a plot count, connectivity and reproducibility — and went 5/5 green on islands with docks at sea and summits on the beach, because it never asked whether an anchor was on land. It also could not have caught the near-identical rivers, because it never compared seeds to each other. Both omissions are now assertions. Any harness used as a gate must first agree with itself across two runs of the same commit before its output counts as evidence.

Build sequence

  1. Author the socket table and a grey-box library — one untextured mesh per piece type.
  2. Build the solver and the gate. Grey-box blockouts across many seeds, reviewed by eye for silhouette, scale and river legibility.
  3. Only once blockouts are approved, author the real pieces and their variants.
  4. Wire scatter, plot survey and bridge quests over the finished grid.

Exit condition: across ten seeds a human can find the dock and the office every time, while silhouette, tier layout, river route and bridge options are obviously different between them — and the mechanical gate is green on all ten.

What St. Mystere is

Purgatory is not a punishment and departure is not a score. St. Mystere used to ferry souls onward once they had accepted what happened. Something broke the island’s system, the office was decommissioned, and now the souls who should have moved on have nowhere to go.

One-sentence promise

Build a village on St. Mystere and help a backlog of stranded souls finish their business before the island forgets how to send them home.

The player gathers resources, restores the post office, builds a place for each spirit to stay, then invites or summons that spirit to the island. Once they arrive, they become a villager with a home, schedule, relationships and a reason to remain.

Albert’s explanation

  • The island once received souls in transit and gave them time to accept their fate.
  • Ferries carried accepted souls onward. The post office handled names, letters and destinations.
  • Decay spread through the office, docks and records until the routes stopped working.
  • The backlog now exists in a place that is almost empty: one ruined post office, one frightened civic clerk, and too many unprocessed arrivals.

Island laws

These rules keep St. Mystere emotionally eerie without making its afterlife arbitrary.

1. St. Mystere receives the unfinished. A soul is routed here when something unresolved still has a claim on its attention.
2. Ferries require acceptance. No soul can be forced across. The ferry moves when the passenger chooses to board.
3. Letters are destination records. They arrive by tide, storm or lantern boat. A letter's contents belong to its addressee: opening someone else's is a breach of custody, not a physical impossibility. The Postmaster holds that custody and does not read what she carries.
4. Decay interrupts the system. Broken records, missing ferrymen and collapsed offices create the backlog; this is a real island crisis, not just metaphor.
5. The dead remain mostly solid. Faces, hands and silhouettes are readable. Translucency appears at edges, in memory spaces and near the water.
6. A home makes arrival possible. A summoned soul needs a prepared place to stay. The island cannot safely receive more residents than it can house.
7. Staying is a valid ending. A soul may decide that finishing its business means building a new life rather than leaving the old one behind.
8. The island’s failure has a cause. Albert knows the office broke, but not what happened to the last proper postmaster or why the far ferry still rings.

St. Mystere’s geography

The island begins almost empty. The post office and dock are the only civic structures still standing; every home and service is restored for a soul the player chooses to summon.

The Arrival Dock

The Postmaster wakes here after the ferry mist takes her consciousness. A voice leads inland.

Gameplay: first controls, tide pools, drift letters and the route to the office.

The Ruined Post Office

Albert’s abandoned workplace is the first safe room and the only functioning address.

Gameplay: ledger, mail sorting, summons, storage and the player’s base.

Empty Home Plots

Cleared places where a future soul can be invited. The first home is humble; later plots can support distinct vocations.

Gameplay: gather resources, build, decorate and summon residents.

The Overgrown Interior

Old paths, broken signs and natural resources remain, but no village life does. The island feels watched without being populated.

Gameplay: gathering, fishing, bugs and clues to the decay.

The Dead Ferry Landing

The departure pier is intact enough to hear bells, but no boat answers. Its restoration is a late-story project.

Gameplay: crossings, acceptance scenes and the backlog’s destination.

The Far Water

The horizon is crowded with faint lights and occasional silhouettes. It is a promise of a route, not a second open world.

Gameplay: threshold mystery and endgame atmosphere.

The recruitment loop

The island’s population is not a background unlock list. Every resident arrives because the player made the island habitable enough to receive them.

Gather → build → summon → live

  • Gather: timber, stone, salvage, plants, fish and tide-washed mail.
  • Build: prepare an empty home or service space suited to a specific soul.
  • Summon: use the repaired post office ledger to invite a named soul from the backlog.
  • Welcome: the soul arrives through the mist, takes possession of its home and becomes a villager.
  • Live: schedules, friendship, errands, festivals, decorating and letters continue as in a cozy life-sim.
  • Finish: help the resident complete their business; they may cross, stay or defer the choice.

Why homes matter

A house is not a reward pedestal. It is a promise that this soul has a place to be while deciding what comes next.

Without a prepared home, a summoned soul cannot stabilize on the island. That keeps the opening empty and eerie, gives building emotional purpose, and makes every new resident visibly change the landscape.

Rule: no new cast members are added to inflate the loop. The frozen eight are deepened through their homes, routines and relationships.

How the letters work

Mail is the story system and the physical reason the Postmaster belongs here. Each delivery changes a resident’s available choices, not just a friendship number.

Where mail comes from

  • The living shore: letters written by people who continued after the resident’s death.
  • The island: notes written by residents before they could admit what they needed.
  • The dead: delayed correspondence from people who crossed earlier.
  • The Post Office: one sealed category with no sender and no delivery date.

What delivery changes

  • Residents gain or lose a daily routine.
  • A locked space may become available.
  • Two residents may finally speak about the same event.
  • The island may gain color, weather, plants, music or a new path.
  • The recipient chooses what to do with the truth.
Design boundary: letters are not a machine for surprise twists. The emotional question is not “what shocking fact was hidden?” but “now that this person knows, what can they choose?”

Story spine

The first story establishes an eerie mystery, then lets ordinary village life become the thing that makes the island worth saving.

Act I · The backlog

A post office with one employee too many

  • The Postmaster travels to her first assignment by ferry and names St. Mystere.
  • The recruiter leaves at his stop; the other passengers disappear into thicker mist.
  • The Postmaster wakes on the dock, follows a voice and finds the ruined office.
  • Albert is the only ghost left, and he is more frightened of her than she is of him.
Act II · The village

Give the stranded somewhere to stay

  • The player gathers resources and repairs the post office’s ledger and summons desk.
  • Each prepared home allows one named soul from the backlog to arrive.
  • New residents create schedules, services, friendships and reasons to explore.
  • The island’s decay appears in missing records, dead ferries and wrong addresses.
Act III · The route

Opening hour: ferry to fog

The intro begins in ordinary travel and ends in a place that should not still be operating.

1. First assignment
The player rides a ferry with another recruit and several ordinary passengers. Their conversation covers the name, the first assignment, why the player became a postmaster and which island they were assigned to.
2. “St. Mystere?”
The recruit reacts to the typed island name: “Whoa. I didn’t know they opened that office back up. It was decommissioned years ago.” He reaches his stop and leaves.
3. The boat empties
A quiet time-lapse carries the remaining passengers off at their stops. The coastline becomes less familiar. The mist thickens beyond the windows.
4. The last crossing
The player hears a voice through the fog, loses consciousness, and wakes alone on St. Mystere’s dock. The ferry and crew are gone.
5. Follow the voice
A distant call leads inland through an empty island toward a single ruined post office. The voice sounds frightened, then disappears whenever the player gets close.
6. Albert
The ghost of Albert emerges from the office. The reveal is eerie for exactly one beat—then Albert panics, apologizes, trips over a ledger and becomes more scared of the Postmaster than she is of him.
7. The backlog
Albert explains the old ferry system, the decay and the stranded souls. The office cannot summon anyone safely until the player repairs a home and restores the summons ledger.
8. First build
The player gathers the first resources, prepares a small house plot and summons the first soul. The island gains its first resident, and the normal life-sim loop begins.
